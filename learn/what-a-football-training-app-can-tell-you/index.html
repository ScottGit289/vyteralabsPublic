What a football training app on your watch can, and can’t, tell you

By Scott Dellow, founder of Vytera Labs and a masters sprinter1 November 20268 min read

Finish a five-a-side session, look at your watch, and it says you covered four kilometres at a gentle pace. It felt nothing like a gentle four kilometres. The watch isn’t wrong about the distance. It is measuring the wrong thing, because football isn’t steady running.

The short version
  • Football is repeated short bursts with recovery in between. Total distance and average pace describe the recovery, not the work.
  • The professional game measures the bursts: sprints, high-intensity distance, and hard accelerations and decelerations, each against a fixed speed or rate threshold.
  • A watch can count the same things by the same definitions. It sees the world once a second where a club’s GPS vest sees it ten times, so it misses the shortest bursts. Compare your sessions with each other, not with a club’s data.
  • The useful question is not “how far did I run?” but “did I do the high-speed work, and is it trending the right way?” That is what protects hamstrings and builds match fitness.

Football has a shape, and distance flattens it

Start from first principles. A runner’s session is close to continuous: speed varies a little around an average, so distance and pace summarise it well. A footballer’s session is the opposite. Most of the time is spent walking, jogging and repositioning. The decisive moments are a few seconds long: a sprint in behind, a press, a recovery run, a sharp stop and turn.

Those bursts are where the physical cost sits. Sprinting loads the hamstrings far beyond jogging. Braking hard from speed loads the quadriceps and tendons eccentrically, which is why the day after a session full of stops and turns hurts more than a longer, steadier run. Average figures dilute all of that into the jogging. Two sessions of the same distance can be completely different: one a steady possession drill, the other a set of repeated sprints. Distance cannot tell them apart.

What the professional game measures instead

Professional clubs have tracked players with GPS vests for well over a decade, and they settled on a small set of metrics, each defined by a threshold. The thresholds vary a little between providers and clubs; the ones below are the most widely used, and they trace back to match-analysis research in the English Premier League.

MetricUsual definitionWhat it tells you
High-intensity distanceDistance covered above 19.8 km/h (5.5 m/s)The volume of genuinely hard running. Often called high-speed running.
SprintsEfforts above about 25 km/h (7 m/s), usually held for at least one secondExposure to near-top speed, the stimulus hamstrings need.
AccelerationsEfforts beyond +3 m/s²Explosive starts, often at speeds that never reach the sprint line.
DecelerationsEfforts beyond −3 m/s²Hard braking: the main source of muscle soreness and tendon load.
Heart rate and zonesTime in each zone, average and maximumThe internal cost of the session, as opposed to the external work.

Two points matter for amateur players. First, these are absolute thresholds. A 25 km/h sprint is quick for a recreational adult, so a good app lets you lower the line, and you should, rather than conclude you never sprint. Second, accelerations matter more as the pitch gets smaller. Research on small-sided games consistently finds that smaller pitches and fewer players produce more accelerations, decelerations and changes of direction, and less high-speed running. Five-a-side is a stop-start workout, not a sprint session, and the numbers should show it.

The honest limit: once a second

This is the part most apps don’t tell you. A Garmin watch app receives one GPS position per second. A professional vest samples at ten times a second, and the research is clear that sampling rate matters most exactly where football lives: short distances, high speeds and rapid changes of speed. Validation studies comparing 1 Hz and higher-rate GPS against timing gates found errors largest over short sprints, and accelerations in particular are hard to measure accurately at low rates.

Work through what that means. An acceleration is a change of speed over time. At one fix a second, a burst that peaks and fades within a second is either invisible or smeared across two readings. So a watch will count fewer accelerations than a vest would for the same session. That isn’t a fault you can fix in software; it is information the watch never received. A trustworthy app says so plainly.

The good news is that consistency is what matters for an individual. The same watch, the same thresholds and the same pitch will under-count by a similar amount every week. So the trend is meaningful even where the absolute number is low. Compare yourself with yourself, not with a Premier League player’s data.

Three practical points follow:

  • Wait for a good fix. Start the session only once the watch says its GPS is ready. The first minutes on a weak fix produce false speed spikes.
  • Use multi-band GPS where your watch has it. Dual-frequency receivers are more accurate near buildings, trees and floodlight pylons, which surround most training pitches.
  • Wear a chest strap for heart rate. Optical sensors on the wrist struggle with the arm movement and rapid heart-rate changes of stop-start sport. A chest strap is markedly more accurate in exactly these conditions.

How to use the numbers well

  1. Track high-speed exposure, not just volume. Studies in field sports have found that players regularly exposed to near-maximal speed in training are at lower risk of hamstring injury than those who rarely reach it. A weekly session that includes some genuine sprints is protective, not risky, provided you build up to it.
  2. Avoid spikes. The risk lies in sudden jumps: a week of nothing followed by a session full of sprints. Use the session history to keep each week within reach of the last.
  3. Read decelerations as recovery debt. A session with many hard decelerations will cost more the next day than its distance suggests. Plan the following day accordingly.
  4. Split the session. Recording each period or drill separately shows where the work happened and whether you faded. A drop in high-intensity distance in the second half is a fitness signal worth acting on.
  5. Set your own thresholds, then leave them alone. Pick a sprint line you reach a few times a session and keep it fixed. Changing thresholds every week destroys the trend, which is the only thing a 1 Hz watch measures reliably.

Training, not match day

One caution. In a match, the referee decides what players may wear. Law 4 of the Laws of the Game allows electronic performance and tracking systems only where the competition permits them, and a referee can ask for any item judged dangerous to be removed. A watch is fine for training, small-sided games and five-a-side leagues that allow it. Don’t assume it is allowed in an eleven-a-side league match.
