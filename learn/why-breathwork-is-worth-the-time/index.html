Why breathwork is worth the time, and where the evidence stops

By Scott Dellow, founder of Vytera Labs and a masters 100m sprinter1 November 202615 min read

Breathing is the only part of your autonomic nervous system you can take over whenever you like. That single fact is why breathwork does anything at all, and it is also why the field is so full of nonsense: a lever anyone can pull attracts a lot of people selling instructions. The physiology underneath is real, well described and in places genuinely counterintuitive. The claims built on top of it vary from solid to invented. Here is where the line sits.

The short version
  • Breathing is the only autonomic function with a voluntary override. Everything else about breathwork follows from that.
  • Your heart rate rises as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out. Lengthening the exhale is a mechanism, not a metaphor.
  • At around six breaths a minute, breathing falls into step with the reflex that regulates blood pressure, and the swing in heart rate becomes very large. This is the best-established effect in the whole field.
  • The urge to breathe comes from rising carbon dioxide, not from falling oxygen. Nearly everything surprising about breath-holding follows from that one fact.
  • Hyperventilating before a breath-hold removes the warning without adding any useful oxygen. That is how strong swimmers drown, and it is the reason this article spends a section on it.
  • The acute physiological effects are well evidenced. The claims about immunity, disease and large performance gains are not.

The only autonomic function you can drive

Your autonomic nervous system runs the things you never think about: heart rate, blood vessel tone, digestion, pupil size, sweating. You cannot decide to dilate a blood vessel. You cannot will your heart to slow. Try to control your own digestion and you will find there is no handle to take hold of.

Breathing is the exception. It runs perfectly well on its own, all night, for your whole life, and yet you can seize control of it mid-sentence and hand it back without a thought. It is the one autonomic process with a voluntary override built in.

That matters because the breathing apparatus is not sealed off from the rest of the autonomic system. It is wired into it. Changing how you breathe changes pressure inside the chest, which changes how much blood returns to the heart, which the pressure sensors in your arteries detect, which changes the signal the brainstem sends back down the vagus nerve to the heart. Breathing is a door into a system you otherwise have no access to, and it is the only one.

The exhale is the half that does the work

Watch a heart rate trace while someone breathes slowly and you will see it rise and fall in time with the breath: faster on the way in, slower on the way out. This is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and despite the name it is entirely normal and a sign of a healthy nervous system rather than a problem.

The mechanism is specific. The vagus nerve applies a continuous brake to the heart's pacemaker. During inspiration that brake is released, and the heart speeds up. During expiration it is reapplied, and the heart slows. So the exhale is not the passive half of the cycle. It is the half where the parasympathetic nervous system is actually doing something measurable.

That is the whole basis of the advice to make your out-breath longer than your in-breath. It is not a soothing image, and it does not need to be believed in to work. If you spend more of each cycle in the phase where the vagal brake is applied, you spend more of your time in the state that brake produces. Protocols built on a 1:2 ratio, four seconds in and eight out, are exploiting exactly this.

Six breaths a minute, and why that number keeps appearing

Almost every slow-breathing protocol you will encounter lands somewhere near six breaths a minute. That is not a coincidence or a tradition. It is a resonance.

Your body regulates blood pressure with the baroreflex: sensors in the carotid arteries and aorta detect pressure changes and the brainstem adjusts heart rate to compensate. Like any control loop with a delay, it takes time to respond — roughly five seconds to correct, so about ten seconds for a full cycle of overshoot and correction. Ten seconds per cycle is 0.1 hertz, which is six cycles a minute.

Breathe at that rate and your breath lines up with the oscillation the baroreflex is already producing. The two reinforce each other and the swing in heart rate becomes far larger than either produces alone — the same phenomenon as pushing a swing in time with its own period.

This is the most robust finding in the field. It is measurable in minutes, it replicates, and it has a mechanism that makes sense. Two honest caveats. First, the exact rate is individual, typically somewhere between four and a half and six and a half breaths a minute, which is why a good protocol lets you adjust it rather than insisting on a single number. Second, the resonance explanation is not entirely settled: work published in 2021 found that a person's resonance frequency is not always stable over time and may be related to heart rate itself. The effect is not in doubt. The full account of why is still being argued about.

And be clear what this gives you: a large heart-rate swing demonstrates that the vagal pathway is responsive. It is a measurement of the system working, not in itself a health outcome.

The urge to breathe has almost nothing to do with oxygen

Hold your breath now and in thirty or forty seconds something will start demanding that you stop. Most people assume that signal means they are running out of oxygen. It does not.

The chemoreceptors that drive ordinary breathing sit in the brainstem and respond to the acidity of the fluid around them, which tracks the carbon dioxide in your blood. Carbon dioxide rises, acidity rises, and the brainstem demands a breath. There are oxygen sensors too, in the carotid bodies, but they stay quiet until blood oxygen falls a long way below normal. In everyday life they contribute very little.

So the discomfort of a breath-hold — the tightness, the involuntary contractions of the diaphragm, the growing insistence — is a carbon dioxide alarm. It is not an oxygen gauge. Your oxygen reserves at that moment are still comfortable.

Once you know this, a lot of things that otherwise look mystical become ordinary engineering. Breath-hold training does not expand your oxygen stores to any meaningful degree. What it shifts is your tolerance of carbon dioxide and your response to the alarm. A trained breath-holder is not someone who needs less oxygen. They are someone whose alarm goes off later and who does not panic when it does. That is what a carbon dioxide tolerance table trains, and it is why progress comes faster than you would expect from something that feels so physical.

The one mistake that kills people

Never practise breath retention in or near water.
  • Hyperventilating before a breath-hold lowers carbon dioxide, which silences the urge to breathe.
  • It adds essentially no oxygen, because your blood is already about 98 per cent saturated.
  • So the warning is removed while the real danger is unchanged. You can reach the point of unconsciousness without ever feeling the need to breathe.
  • Underwater, that is drowning. It happens to strong swimmers, confident ones, in shallow water, with no struggle for anyone to notice.

This is the direct, lethal consequence of the previous section, and it deserves its own heading because the people it kills are not careless. They are fit swimmers doing breath-hold lengths in a pool, often competitive ones, who have hyperventilated first precisely because it lets them go further.

The mechanism was described in the Journal of Applied Physiology in 1961 by Albert Craig, investigating why swimmers were losing consciousness underwater. It has been confirmed many times since and the physiology is not controversial. Hyperventilation blows off carbon dioxide. The carbon dioxide alarm is what would otherwise force you to the surface while you still had oxygen to spare. Remove the alarm and oxygen keeps falling in silence until the brain stops. There is no gasping and no panic. Witnesses routinely report that the swimmer simply stopped moving.

It is called shallow-water blackout because it often happens in the last few metres of an ascent, where falling ambient pressure drops the oxygen in the lungs further still. But the pool floor is quite enough. Recent work has found that hyperventilating for as little as thirty seconds before a breath-hold measurably delays the carbon dioxide response, and that fasting makes it worse.

On dry land, lying down, the same physiology is survivable: if you lose consciousness you start breathing again, because the brainstem takes over the moment you stop interfering. In water you inhale. This is why every protocol involving retention should be done sitting or lying, never standing — a faint while standing is a head injury rather than a moment of dizziness — and never, under any circumstances, in or near water.

Why your hands tingle, and why that is not a sign it is working

Anyone who has done a round of fast, deep breathing knows the sensations: light-headedness, tingling in the fingers and around the mouth, sometimes the hands cramping into a claw. These get described in some quarters as energy moving, or as a sign the practice is taking effect. They are neither. They are hypocapnia — low carbon dioxide — and the physiology is well understood.

Carbon dioxide is mildly acidic in solution, so blowing a lot of it off makes the blood more alkaline. Blood vessels in the brain constrict, which produces the light-headedness; and the shift in acidity changes how much calcium is freely available to nerves, which makes them fire more readily. That is the tingling, and in its stronger form the cramping in hands and feet.

None of this is dangerous in a seated, dry-land session, and it passes within a minute of breathing normally. But it is a predictable chemical consequence, not an achievement. A protocol encouraging you to chase those sensations is encouraging you to chase a side effect.

What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Here is where I will be blunter than most writing on this subject, because the gap between what is demonstrated and what is claimed is wide.

Well supported. That slow breathing produces immediate, measurable changes in heart rate variability and in the balance of autonomic activity. That breathing near the resonance rate produces much larger heart-rate oscillations than ordinary breathing. That the acute effects are reliable and reproduce across studies. The 2017 review in Breathe by Russo and colleagues is a good survey of the physiology, and Zaccaro and colleagues' 2018 systematic review covers the psychophysiological side.

Moderately supported. That training with heart-rate variability biofeedback reduces self-reported stress and anxiety. There are a number of trials and several meta-analyses, generally finding modest benefits. The honest caveat is that most are small, few are properly blinded — it is hard to blind someone to how they are breathing — and the outcomes are usually questionnaires. A real effect of modest size is the fair reading.

Weakly supported or unsupported. Much of the rest. Claims that breathwork modulates the immune system rest on a very small number of studies with small samples. Claims about curing or managing specific diseases are not established. Claims of substantial improvements in endurance or sprint performance are not well supported; breathing protocols are not a meaningful ergogenic aid on current evidence. Anything described as detoxification is not a physiological concept.

This should not read as dismissal. An intervention that reliably shifts your autonomic state within two minutes, costs nothing, needs no equipment and has no side effects worth the name is genuinely useful. It just is not what it is often sold as, and the overselling does the real thing no favours.

If you train

I am a masters sprinter, so this is the part I care about personally, and it is the part where I have had to be most careful not to believe what I wanted to.

Breathing protocols will not make you faster. There is no good evidence that they improve sprint performance, and I would be suspicious of anyone telling you otherwise. What they are useful for is arousal control, which is a different claim and a more defensible one.

Sprinting badly is very often an arousal problem. Too flat and you are slow out of the blocks. Too wound up and you tighten, which in a 100m is the difference between running through the line and tying up at eighty metres. The physiology above gives you a direct way to move in either direction: a few slow breaths with a long exhale to come down, faster and shallower to come up. On a start line, with ten minutes to go, that is a real and usable tool.

The other use is between efforts. How quickly your heart rate comes down after a hard rep is a reasonable marker of parasympathetic recovery, and slow breathing accelerates it. Whether that produces a better session is harder to show.

And for anyone over 40 reading this with longevity in mind: the fair statement is that higher heart rate variability is associated with better cardiovascular outcomes, and that slow breathing acutely increases it. Whether training that number changes your outcomes is not established. Association is not the same as a lever, and I would rather say so than imply otherwise.

How to start, if you want to

Nothing here requires an app, a course or a teacher. If you do one thing: sit down, breathe in for four seconds and out for six, for five minutes. That is six breaths a minute with an exhale bias — the resonance effect and the vagal effect together, and most of the available benefit.

If you want more structure, box breathing — four in, four hold, four out, four hold — is the easiest to remember and the hardest to get wrong, because it involves no hyperventilation and no extended retention. It is the one I would give to anyone starting.

If you go on to breath-holds, three rules. Sit or lie down, every time. Never in or near water, ever. And do not chase a number: the point is the carbon dioxide tolerance, and that moves on its own if you practise consistently.

Where this comes from

  • The physiology of slow breathing in healthy people: Russo, Santarelli and O'Rourke, "The physiological effects of slow breathing in the healthy human" (Breathe, 2017, volume 13, pages 298–309).
  • Psychophysiological effects of slow breathing: Zaccaro and colleagues, "How breath-control can change your life: a systematic review on psycho-physiological correlates of slow breathing" (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2018).
  • Resonance, the baroreflex and heart-rate variability biofeedback: Lehrer and Gevirtz, "Heart rate variability biofeedback: how and why does it work?" (Frontiers in Psychology, 2014), building on Vaschillo and colleagues' earlier work on resonance frequency.
  • The caveat on resonance frequency not being stable over time: a 2021 study in Scientific Reports relating it to the inter-beat interval.
  • Loss of consciousness during breath-hold swimming: Craig, "Causes of loss of consciousness during underwater swimming" (Journal of Applied Physiology, 1961), and the substantial literature on hypoxic blackout since.
  • Hyperventilation before apnoea and blackout risk: recent work on the effects of hyperventilation on repeated breath-holding, including in the fasted state (2024).

This article is general information about breathing practice, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat anything. Breath retention in particular carries real risk. Speak to a doctor before using breath-hold or hyperventilation protocols if you are pregnant, or if you have a cardiovascular condition, high or low blood pressure, a history of fainting, epilepsy or any seizure disorder, a respiratory condition including asthma or COPD, a history of stroke or aneurysm, or if you are recovering from surgery. Never practise breath retention in or near water.
