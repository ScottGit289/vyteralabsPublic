Why anonymity makes online dating safer

By Scott Dellow, founder of Vytera Labs and a global IT executive1 November 20267 min read

Dating apps ask you to trust a stranger quickly, and the first thing most people hand over is the one piece of information that unlocks everything else: a phone number. Anonymity is not about hiding who you are. It is about choosing when a stranger stops being one.

The short version
  • A phone number is no longer a contact detail. It is a key to your bank, your logins, your name, your photo and, in many places, your address.
  • Most of the harm in online dating starts with someone you have only just met knowing more about you than they have earned: images you did not ask for, harassment that follows you off the app, scams that depend on knowing who you are.
  • Anonymity is not deception. You can be completely honest with a person while giving the systems around the conversation nothing to look up.
  • Disclose in layers: the app, then a code you can discard, then a number, then a surname, then a place, each step after the last one has been earned.
  • It cuts both ways. The other person has given you nothing either, so meet in public, tell someone where you are, and never send money.

What a phone number is now

Twenty years ago a phone number let someone ring you. Today it is the recovery address for your email, the second factor on your bank, and the lookup key on the big messaging apps, which will show your name and your photo to anyone who saves your number. In the United States, and increasingly elsewhere, it is also the search term on people-finder sites that can return a full name, an age, relatives and a home address for a small fee. Hand it to a stranger on a Tuesday and by Wednesday they can know your surname, your employer and your street without you having said any of it.

It is also permanent reach. Block someone on a dating app and they are gone. Block someone who has your number and they are one cheap SIM away from being back. And the number itself can be taken from you: a SIM-swap attack, where a fraudster talks your carrier into moving your number to their phone, hands them every login that number protects. The more people who hold your number, the more places it can leak from.

Where the harm actually starts

The dating platforms' own safety pages say the same thing, and it is worth noticing: keep the conversation on the platform until you trust the person. They say it because they see what happens when people do not. Pew Research Center's surveys of online daters have repeatedly found that a majority of younger women have been sent a sexually explicit message or image they did not ask for, and that a meaningful minority of all online daters have been threatened with physical harm. Romance fraud, where someone builds a relationship over weeks and then asks for money, costs victims tens of millions of pounds a year in the UK according to Action Fraud, and hundreds of millions of dollars a year in the US according to the FBI, and both agencies say most of it is never reported.

Every one of those harms is easier when the other person has your real identity and a permanent way to reach you. Harassment follows a phone number from app to app. An intimate image is far more dangerous when it can be tied to a real name and a real employer; that is the whole mechanism of the threat. A scam works by learning enough about you to be convincing, and a person who already knows your surname, your job and your town has a head start. Anonymity does not stop a bad actor existing. It limits what they can do with you, and it means that when you walk away, you have actually left.

Anonymity is not deception

There is an understandable objection here. Isn't anonymity exactly what the catfish and the scammers use? No. A catfish lies about who they are. Anonymity is something different: the systems around the conversation, the network, the servers, the data brokers, learn nothing, while the person you are talking to learns exactly what you choose to tell them, truthfully.

You can give your real first name, describe your real job and send a photo that actually looks like you, and still have handed over nothing that can be searched, sold or used to find you after you have said goodbye. The distinction is between what a person knows and what a database knows. Good dating depends on the first. Most of the harm comes from the second. Being private about your identifiers while being honest about yourself is not a contradiction; it is how most people already behave in a bar, where nobody expects your surname and address before the second drink.

Disclose in layers

The practical version of all this is a simple order of operations. Each step is earned by the one before it.

  1. On the app. A first name and photos. Use pictures that are not already on your work profile or public social accounts, because a reverse image search turns a photo into a name in seconds. Keep the location off your pictures.
  2. Off the app, still anonymous. At some point the app's own chat is not enough: you want to talk properly, send a photo that is not a profile picture, or just stop checking a dating app. Move to a messenger that does not need your phone number, and swap a code or a handle you can throw away. You get a real conversation, and nothing that can be looked up.
  3. Your number, after you have met. Once you have been in the same room and still like each other, a phone number is a reasonable thing to share. Before that it is a liability with no benefit you cannot get another way.
  4. Your surname, your workplace, your home. In that order, and slowly. Before each one, search yourself with what they would then know and see what it unlocks. That is the test.

Two habits make every layer safer. Set messages to expire, so that a lost phone, a stolen one or a breach at the other end does not expose months of history. And treat photographs as the most permanent thing you send: anything that reaches another person's screen can be captured there, by any app, and no software can promise otherwise.

It cuts both ways

Everything above applies to the person you are talking to. They are anonymous as well, and that is a reason for ordinary caution rather than alarm.

  • Video before you meet. A five-minute call confirms a face matches its photos and costs nothing.
  • Public place, own transport. Arrive and leave under your own steam, and tell a friend where you are and who you are meeting.
  • Never send money. Not a loan, not a gift card, not cryptocurrency, however good the story or however long the relationship. The story is the method.
  • Watch for pressure, not platforms. Someone who insists on moving quickly to a channel where they cannot be reported, or who will not video call, is telling you something.
  • Use the tools. Block and report, on the dating app and on whatever messenger you move to. Reporting is what gets the next person protected.
