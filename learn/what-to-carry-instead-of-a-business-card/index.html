The business card isn’t dead. The paper is.

By Scott Dellow, founder of Vytera Labs and a global IT executive31 October 20267 min read

Every conference still ends the same way: a pocket of cards, most of which will never be typed into a phone. The failure is not the idea of a business card. It is the medium, and the fix is not an app the other person has to download.

The short version
  • A paper card hands the work to the other person. Most of that work never gets done, and the card is out of date the day your title changes.
  • Most digital business card apps move the problem rather than solve it: the other person needs the app, or your details sit on the vendor's server behind a subscription.
  • Every modern phone camera already reads a QR code, and every phone address book already understands the vCard contact format. Put one inside the other and the other person needs nothing at all.
  • A watch is a good place for that code: it is already on your wrist, your phone stays in your pocket, and you can check what you are about to share before you share it.

Why paper fails at the moment that matters

A business card is a request. It asks the person you just met to do something later: find the card, open their contacts, type your name, number, email and company, get them right, and remember who you were. That is four or five minutes of admin for a contact they may not yet value, performed at the end of a long day. Most of us know what happens next, because we have a drawer full of other people's cards to prove it.

Paper has two other problems. It is static, so it is wrong the moment anything changes, and senior careers change more than most: a new title, a board seat, a second company. And it is one-directional. You give something; whether anything comes back depends entirely on their effort. The card you really want is one where the contact is created there and then, in front of you, with nothing to type.

Why most digital business cards move the problem

Digital business card apps have existed for years, and most of them share one of two designs. Either both people need the same app, which fails the moment the other person does not have it, or your card is a web page hosted by the vendor, and the scan opens that page. The second design works on any phone, but look at what you have agreed to. Your contact details live on someone else's server. The vendor can see who scanned you and when. The card usually comes with a monthly fee, and the day you stop paying, every card you ever handed out stops working.

Neither of those is a reason to go back to paper. They are reasons to want the third design, which has been available for years and which almost nobody uses: a plain QR code that carries the contact itself.

The standard that already does the job

Two pieces of plumbing make this possible, and both are already in every pocket.

The first is the QR code. iPhone and Android camera apps read them natively; nobody installs a scanner any more. The second is vCard, the contact-file format that address books have exchanged for decades. It is an open standard, published by the Internet Engineering Task Force, and when a phone camera sees a QR code containing a vCard it does the obvious thing: it offers to save the contact. Name, title, company, mobile, email, website, LinkedIn, all filled in, one tap to confirm.

Notice what is not in that chain. There is no app for the other person. There is no server, because the code is the card. There is no account and nothing to expire. And nothing is logged, because nobody is in the middle. The data goes from your screen to their camera and nowhere else.

Why the wrist

You could put that code on your phone's lock screen, and some people do. A watch is a better home for three reasons.

  • It is already out. Reaching for a phone mid-conversation changes the conversation. Turning your wrist does not.
  • Their phone, not yours. The person saving the contact is the one holding their phone. You stay hands-free.
  • You can check first. A good implementation lets you see the text of what is about to be shared before anyone scans it. That matters when you carry more than one identity: the Work card for the client, the Board card for the other non-executives, the Personal card for the people you would rather give a mobile than a switchboard.

A small screen does impose discipline. A QR code has a size limit before the squares become too small for a phone camera to read from a normal distance, and a round watch face can only show the square that fits inside the circle. A card that respects that, keeps the code pure black on white, and trims the least essential fields first if your details run long will scan from a normal conversational distance. A styled, branded code that does not scan is a business card nobody receives.

How to use one well

  1. Offer, don't present. "Shall I give you my details?" then turn your wrist. The novelty does the rest; people lean in.
  2. Hold it at arm's length, not under their nose. Phone cameras focus poorly very close up. Fifteen to twenty-five centimetres, screen square to the camera, brightness up.
  3. Match the card to the room. A LinkedIn-only code is much smaller and scans faster across a crowded reception. The full contact card is for the conversation that earned it.
  4. Check what you are sharing. If your watch can preview the card, look at it before the first scan of the day. The wrong profile at the wrong table is the digital equivalent of handing over an old card.
  5. Remember a code is public. Anything in a QR code can be read by anyone who can see the screen, including a Wi-Fi password if you choose to share one that way. Show it when you mean to.
