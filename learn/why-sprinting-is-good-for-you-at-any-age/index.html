Why sprinting is good for you, whatever your age

By Scott Dellow, founder of Vytera Labs and a masters 100m sprinter31 October 20269 min read

Most people stop sprinting at about the age they leave school, and most advice for staying healthy after 40 never mentions it again. That is a mistake. Ageing takes your power before it takes your strength, and sprinting is the most direct way to train exactly the things that go first.

The short version
  • With age you lose fast-twitch muscle fibre faster than slow-twitch, so power declines roughly twice as fast as strength. Power is what gets you up stairs, out of a chair and back on your feet when you trip.
  • Sprinting is one of the few activities that fully recruits those fast fibres. Masters sprinters in their 60s and 70s keep muscle and power that sedentary people half their age have already lost.
  • The impact of sprinting loads bone in the way bone responds to. Short hard efforts also improve heart and metabolic fitness in a fraction of the time of steady running.
  • The risks are real but specific: hamstrings, calves and Achilles, and the heart if you go from nothing to flat out. Every one of them is managed by building up over weeks, not days.
  • You do not need a track, a club or talent. You need a flat strip of grass, a warm-up and patience.

What ageing actually takes from you

Muscle is not lost evenly. The fibres that go first are the type II fibres, the fast-twitch ones that produce force quickly. Classic muscle biopsy work found that a thigh muscle in an 80-year-old had roughly half the fibres it had at 20, with the fast fibres hit hardest. The result shows up in how you move long before it shows up on a scan: studies of older adults have measured leg power falling at around twice the yearly rate of leg strength.

That matters because almost everything that keeps you independent is a power problem, not a strength problem. Catching yourself when you stumble, crossing a road before the lights change, getting off the floor, climbing stairs with shopping. None of these is about how much you can lift slowly. They are about how fast you can apply force. Walking, cycling and most gym machines barely touch the fast fibres, because they never ask the muscle to produce force quickly. Use it or lose it is literal here: fibres that are never recruited are the ones the body lets go.

Sprinting trains the fibres that are disappearing

A maximal sprint recruits essentially every motor unit you own, including the fast ones nothing else reaches. That is why sprinting is such a precise answer to the problem above. You are not training generally and hoping some of it lands on the fast fibres. You are training them directly.

The clearest evidence comes from the people who never stopped. Studies of masters sprinters, athletes competing in their 60s, 70s and beyond, consistently find more fast-twitch fibre, greater muscle cross-sectional area and far higher leg power than in sedentary people of the same age. Their performance declines with age, as everyone's does, but from a much higher base and more slowly. Some of that is selection; the people still sprinting at 70 are not a random sample. But intervention studies in previously untrained older adults point the same way: when training includes fast, high-force work, power improves at any age.

This is the argument in one line. If you want to keep the capacity that ageing attacks first, you have to keep using it, and sprinting is the purest way to use it.

Bone likes impact

Bone adapts to the loads placed on it, and it responds most to loads that are high, fast and a little unusual. Sprinting delivers exactly that. Each ground contact at speed puts a force through the leg several times your body weight, over a few hundredths of a second.

Comparisons of masters athletes by event are telling. Sprinters and jumpers tend to have the densest and strongest leg bones; endurance runners and race walkers much less so, sometimes no better than people who do not train at all. Endurance volume is not what bone is asking for. Short, sharp, high-force loading is. For anyone thinking about bone health in their 50s and beyond, that is a strong reason to keep some fast running in the week.

A big return on a small amount of time

Sprinting is also a cardiovascular and metabolic stimulus, and an unusually efficient one. Research on sprint interval training over the past twenty years has repeatedly found that a few minutes of truly hard effort per week, spread across short repetitions with full rest, produces improvements in aerobic capacity and insulin sensitivity comparable to much longer blocks of steady endurance work. The total time spent actually sprinting in a session is often under a minute.

Short maximal efforts also produce a sharp hormonal response. One widely cited study found that a single 30-second all-out effort raised growth hormone markedly for over an hour afterwards. Be careful with what that means: an acute hormone spike is not the same as a long-term health outcome, and anyone promising you youth from a hormone graph is selling something. What it does show is that the body treats a sprint as a serious signal, which is why so little of it goes so far.

The part nobody measures

Sprinting is also a skill, and learning a skill in your 40s, 50s or 60s is good for you in ways that are harder to quantify. Starting, accelerating and running tall at speed demand coordination, timing and attention. There is a target in front of you every session: a time, a distance, a stride count. Masters athletics gives that target structure, with five-year age groups from 35 upwards and age-graded tables that let a 58-year-old compare a 100m time fairly against a 28-year-old's. Few forms of exercise offer that kind of honest, measurable progress at an age when most people have stopped expecting to get better at anything physical.

The honest part: what can go wrong

Sprinting carries risks, and they are specific enough to plan for.

  • Hamstrings. The most common sprint injury at any age, and more so after 40. Almost always a product of going too fast too soon, running flat out when tired, or skipping the warm-up.
  • Calves and Achilles. Tendons adapt more slowly than muscle and more slowly still with age. They need weeks of gradually increasing load before they are ready for maximal speed.
  • Your heart. A maximal sprint is a maximal cardiovascular effort. If you are over 40, have been inactive, or have any history of heart disease, high blood pressure or symptoms on exertion, see a doctor before you start. This is not a formality.
  • Falls. Rare in practice, but choose a flat, even surface with nothing to trip on while you relearn running fast.

Every one of these is managed the same way: build up over weeks, never sprint tired, and treat the first two months as preparation rather than training. People get hurt sprinting when they treat it as something they can simply go and do. Treated as a skill to rebuild, it is remarkably safe.

How to start, at 30, 50 or 70

  1. Get cleared if you need to. See the heart point above. Then commit to eight weeks before you run anything near flat out.
  2. Walk and jog first, then add strides. After a warm-up, run 4 to 6 relaxed efforts of 50 to 60 metres at perhaps 70% of what feels like your top speed, walking back fully between each. Two sessions a week. Do this for two to three weeks.
  3. Add hills. A gentle hill of 30 to 40 metres lets you work hard with shorter strides and less hamstring strain. Hill sprints are how many coaches introduce speed safely.
  4. Build speed gradually. Move from 70% to 80% to 90% over the following weeks, one step at a time. Keep every effort short, 30 to 60 metres, and rest until you feel completely recovered, which at full speed means several minutes. Sprinting is quality work; if you are breathing hard when you start the next one, you waited too little.
  5. Stop while you are fresh. The session is over when your speed drops or your form goes, never when you are exhausted. Six good efforts beat twelve tired ones, and the tired ones are where hamstrings go.
  6. Strength work in the week. Two short sessions of squats, hinges, calf raises and hamstring work support everything above and are themselves part of the anti-ageing case.
  7. Watch recovery. Sleep, resting heart rate and how your legs feel tell you when to sprint and when to hold back. Sprinting on a bad night's sleep is how a good month becomes six weeks off.

Progress is not linear and it is slower after 50 than it was at 20. But it is there, and it is measurable, which brings us to the last point.

Where this comes from

  • Muscle fibre loss with age and the preferential loss of type II fibres: Lexell and colleagues' biopsy studies of the vastus lateralis across adult life (Journal of the Neurological Sciences, 1988).
  • Power declining faster than strength in older adults: Skelton and colleagues (Age and Ageing, 1994) and later work on leg power and functional ability.
  • Muscle and power in masters sprinters: Korhonen and colleagues (Journal of Applied Physiology, 2006) and related Finnish masters-athlete studies.
  • Bone in masters athletes by event: Wilks and colleagues (Bone, 2009), comparing sprinters, distance runners, race walkers and controls.
  • Sprint interval training versus endurance training: Gibala, Little and colleagues (Journal of Physiology, 2006 onwards).
  • Growth hormone response to a single 30-second sprint: Stokes and colleagues (Journal of Applied Physiology, 2002).

This article is general information about training, not medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are over 40 and have been inactive, or get symptoms on exertion, talk to a doctor before starting sprint training.
