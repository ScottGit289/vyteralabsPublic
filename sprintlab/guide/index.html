SprintLab

User Guide

Publisher: Vytera Labs Ltd · For: SprintLab on iPhone · support@vyteralabs.com

SprintLab times your sprints from video, gives you a programme to get faster, keeps your times honestly, tells you each morning what kind of session your body is ready for, and finds the patterns in your own data that explain your best days. Everything runs on your phone — there is no account and nothing to sign in to.

If you read nothing else
  • Start with a speed test: film one sprint over a measured 20 or 30 metres and the app times it frame by frame.
  • The whole app works on manual entry. A wearable only auto-fills numbers you could type yourself.
  • Every metric is labelled with where it came from, so you always know what a score is built on.
  • Timing methods and start types are kept in separate record books — a hand time can never overwrite an electronic one.
  • Your data lives only on this device. Export a backup periodically; nobody else holds a copy.

2. Your first speed test

A new install opens on a short speed test, because one number of your own is worth more than any amount of setup. You can skip it and come back from Home → Time a sprint.

  • Mark out the distance. 30 metres is the standard test; choose 20 if space is tight. Measure it with a tape or use pitch markings, and put a cone or bag at each end.
  • Set the phone side-on to the finish, propped up and level with the line, far enough back that the start is in shot. It must not move.
  • Film in the Camera app. Normal Video at 60 fps is ideal; Slo-mo also works. Press record, walk to the start, pause, then sprint through the finish.
  • Choose the clip and mark two frames, as described below.

Your 7 free days start when you save that first time, not before.

3. Timing a sprint on video

Open Tools → Video sprint timing and tap Choose a clip. The system photo picker opens; SprintLab receives only the clip you pick.

  • Find the frame. Drag the bar for a rough position, then step with −10, −1, +1, +10 frames. Play at full, half or quarter speed if that helps.
  • Mark start. For a standing start, the first frame in which you move. For a flying sprint, the frame your chest crosses the start line.
  • Mark finish. The frame your chest crosses the finish line.
  • Set the distance you measured, and save.

The app shows your time, your average speed and the precision of the measurement, which is about one frame: roughly ±0.02 s at 60 frames a second. A flying sprint over 10 or 20 metres measures your top speed; a standing start measures acceleration. Results are saved to your personal bests as Video timed.

After saving you see a result card with your time, speed and level over a still from your clip. Share result sends it as an image; Challenge a friend sends it with a link.

4. The 8-week speed programme

Tools → 8-week speed programme. Three sessions a week — acceleration, max velocity and speed endurance — each with a warm-up, the work, the rest between reps and one coaching cue.

  • Day 1 is a test over 20 or 30 metres. A test you ran in the last 7 days counts, and the programme is dated from it.
  • Weeks 1–3 build, week 4 eases off and retests, weeks 5–7 raise the intensity, and week 8 tapers into a final retest.
  • Your three tests sit side by side on the programme screen, with the improvement since day 1.

Marking a session done also writes it to your training log. Leave at least a day between sessions, and stop if you feel a muscle tighten or a sharp pain.

5. Levels, your week and challenges

Speed levels

A 20 or 30 metre result from a standing or block start places you on an eight-level ladder, from Starter to Elite. Home shows your level and exactly how much faster you need to run to reach the next one. The ladder is SprintLab's own scale, not a ranking against other people. Flying sprints and other distances carry no level, because a level has to mean the same thing every time.

Your week

The goal is three training days a week, and Home counts weeks in a row. It is deliberately not a daily streak: speed is built on rest days too, so a rest day costs you nothing, and when the Ready tab advises rest the card tells you the week is still on track.

Challenges

On any result, Challenge a friend shares your card with a link that sets your time as their target. Opening a challenge link shows the time to beat; accept it and the app takes you to the video timer. Home shows how far you are from the target, and tells you when you have beaten it.

  • Like with like. A challenge carries how the run started. A flying sprint is beaten by a flying sprint, and a standing start by a standing or block start.
  • Telling your challenger. When you beat a challenge, Tell them you beat it opens the share sheet with your result and a message for you to send. The link in it makes your time their new target. SprintLab has no accounts or servers, so it cannot notify anyone itself: you choose who receives it.
  • Cancelling. Cancel challenge on the Home card drops a challenge you no longer want. Your results are kept.

6. Setting up your athlete profile

There is no sign-up step — open the app and it is ready. Start with Settings → Edit athlete profile so age grading and the readiness baselines have something to work from.

Then log one thing you already know: a recent personal best, or yesterday's session. Every analysis in the app is built from your own logged data rather than assumptions about an average athlete, so it gets sharper the more you give it.

7. Performance Readiness

Each day the Ready tab produces a 0–100 readiness score and one plain training call.

What the call means

ScoreCallWhat it means
80–100SprintPrimed for quality speed work or a time trial.
60–79GymGood for strength and power work; hold back top-end speed volume.
40–59RecoveryKeep it light — tempo, technique or mobility only.
Below 40RestPrioritise rest or very light movement today.

Two rules are worth knowing. If you have logged an injury or niggle in the last few days, the app will never tell you to sprint. And if it has no recovery data at all to read, it defaults to Gym rather than guessing at speed work.

What goes into the score

It fuses whatever it has, and the weights renormalise over the inputs that are actually present — so a fully manual day still gets a real score:

  • Your wearable's own recovery or readiness score, and its sleep score — or the hours you slept, when there is no sleep score (Apple Health).
  • Sleep you rated yourself when logging a session, from 1 (poor) to 5 (great).
  • HRV and resting heart rate, each compared against your personal baseline rather than a population average.
  • Body temperature deviation and stress balance.
  • Your training load — the last 7 days against your 28-day weekly average, so a recent spike shows up.
  • Your own check-in on leg freshness, energy and motivation, each 1–10.
  • Whether you have reported a recent injury.

The dashboard explains itself rather than just showing a number. Under a heading such as Why you're ready or Why to hold back today, it lists what moved your score: each signal with its reading, how it compares with your own baseline, and the points it adds or takes off — for example “Sleep: 5.3 h, a short night, −9” or “HRV: 48 ms, 14% below your baseline, −6”. Points are measured from a typical day, which scores 70, so they add up to your score.

Signals within a few percent of your baseline are listed as close to usual rather than flagged. If today rests on very little — a single check-in, say — the score is held closer to typical and the screen says so, along with what was not counted.

Tap any metric to enter or edit it. The score updates immediately.

8. Where each number comes from

Every metric carries its source, so you can judge how much to trust a score:

SourceMeaning
ManualYou typed it. A manual value always wins.
Oura, AppleSynced from that source.
CalculatedDerived from other values you have supplied.
EstimatedInferred. The weakest of the four — treat a score built mostly on estimates with proportionate caution.

When you edit a synced metric by hand, the original reading is kept underneath, so you can put it back.

9. Connecting a wearable

Entirely optional. SprintLab supports Apple Health and Oura. Go to Settings → Manage wearables.

Apple Health is read natively on the device through HealthKit. Oura uses its own secure sign-in, so SprintLab never sees your password, and your health data comes straight to your phone.

Once connected you can Sync now at any time, or leave it on an automatic schedule. Disconnect whenever you like. For Apple Health you can also review or revoke access in iOS Settings → Privacy & Security → Health.

10. Personal bests

Quick-pick distances run 20m, 30m, 50m, 60m, 80m, 100m, 150m, 200m, 300m and 400m, and you can add custom distances for sessions that do not fit a track.

Records are kept in separate books

A record is identified by distance and start type and timing method together. That is deliberate: comparing a flying 30 with a block 30, or a stopwatch time with electronic timing, tells you nothing.

Wind

Record the wind with the time; a tailwind is positive. For sprints up to and including 200m, a tailwind over +2.0 m/s makes the time wind-aided: it is kept and flagged, but never becomes a legal personal best. Wind is irrelevant to 300m and 400m.

One nuance worth knowing: if you leave the wind blank it counts as legal. The app will not invalidate a time on a reading you never took, so record the wind when you have it.

You can hide wind-aided results entirely with Settings → Show wind-aided results.

When you set a personal best, the app tells you exactly which time you beat.

11. The training log

Log a session from the Train tab: session type (Sprint, Gym, Tempo, Recovery or Other), timed reps, gym work, notes, injuries and sleep quality.

Out of that you get progress graphs per distance, PB history with improvement deltas, an improving/stable/declining trend, weekly session volume, and a 0–100 consistency score that rewards showing up week after week rather than one heroic session a month.

Logging sleep quality and any niggle matters more than it looks: both feed tomorrow's readiness score.

12. Analytics

SprintLab compares your recovery with how fast you actually ran. Each day's best run is measured against your own usual time at that distance, so a 30 metre day is never compared with a 200 metre day, and video-timed results count alongside session reps.

An insight appears only when there is evidence for it: at least eight days that have both a recovery reading and a timed sprint, a clear relationship, and a real gap between your better and worse days. It then tells you the size of the effect, the line it falls either side of and how many days it rests on — for example “You run about 1.8% faster on days your sleep is above 7.4 h (9 days against 8)”. With fewer than 14 days it is labelled an early signal.

It also shows what your recovery looked like on the days a personal best fell, which tells you what to reproduce before a race. Only signals you can act on are used — sleep, HRV, resting heart rate, recovery, stress and your check-in.

Until there is enough data, the screen tells you how many more days it needs. That is honest rather than broken.

13. Sprint calculator and age grading

Sprint calculator

Average speed in m/s, km/h and mph (choose which one leads elsewhere in the app with Settings → Speed shown in); equal-speed equivalents across 30, 60, 100, 150, 200, 300 and 400m; and even-pace split targets.

Age grading

WMA-style age-graded scoring for 60m, 100m, 200m and 400m, computed entirely offline from approximate tables. Useful for masters athletes, when the raw clock stops being the interesting number. Other distances are not age-graded.

14. On-device intelligence

Settings → Use on-device intelligence lets Apple Intelligence write your insights and daily briefing. It runs entirely on your iPhone: nothing is sent to Apple, to us, or to anyone else, and it is never used for advertising or model training.

It needs a compatible iPhone with Apple Intelligence enabled. On any other device — or if the model is still downloading — SprintLab uses its built-in coach instead and works exactly the same. The app tells you which one produced what you are reading; the fallback is never silent.

16. Backup, restore and delete

Your data is stored on your device, so backing it up is your job rather than ours. That is the direct cost of there being no server, and it is worth stating plainly.

  • Export backup hands a copy to the iOS share sheet — Files, AirDrop or email.
  • Import backup asks Restore from this backup? and offers Replace my data or Keep my data. Read that prompt carefully: Replace discards what is currently on the device.
  • Delete all data confirms twice — it is permanent. Deleting the app removes everything too.

Export a backup periodically. There is no cloud copy and nobody else holds one for you.

17. When something is wrong

SprintLab collects no diagnostics and sends us nothing, so a report is only as good as what you tell us. Include your device model and iOS version (iOS Settings → General → About), the SprintLab version from its own Settings screen, what you did, what you expected, and what happened instead. A screenshot helps if it is something you can see.

Please do not send your backup export unless we ask — it contains your whole training history. If a problem genuinely needs it we will say so, and explain what we will do with it and when we will delete it.
