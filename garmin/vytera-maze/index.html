01

The round-screen problem

The arcade maze is 28 × 31 tiles in a 4:3 frame. Dropped onto a circular 416 × 416 display it either loses its corners to the bezel or shrinks until the pellets are a pixel wide. Neither is playable.

So this board is purpose-built: a 19 × 19 grid with its corner cells marked void, giving a maze that reads as a disc and uses the round screen instead of fighting it. It keeps what makes the genre work — a sealed ghost pen at the centre, side tunnels that wrap, power pellets at the four widest points — at 150 pellets rather than 244, which is about seventy seconds a level. Right for a watch.

The layout is mirror-symmetric, every open cell is reachable, and no 2 × 2 block is fully open — a maze with an open square in it has no corridors, only a room. All three are asserted by a tool that reads the board out of the source, so the checks cannot drift from what ships.

02

Four ghosts, four different rules

The ghosts do not share one chase. Each hunts by its own rule, which is what turns a maze into a game.

The red one

Comes straight at you. It targets the tile you are standing on, so it is always behind you and always closing.

The pink one

Aims four tiles ahead of you rather than at you, to cut you off at the next junction instead of following you into it.

The cyan one

Targets the red ghost's position reflected through the tile two ahead of you — so it gets more dangerous the closer the red one already is.

The orange one

Chases until it is within eight tiles, then breaks for its own corner. The one you can bait, if you know it.

They alternate between scattering and chasing on a fixed schedule, and reverse on the spot whenever the mode flips — the tell that the hunt has just changed. A power pellet frightens them, and eating them inside one frightened window scores 200, then 400, then 800, then 1600.

03

Speed, and one deliberate change

Ghosts run at 0.67× your speed on level 1, rising to 0.92× by level 6, and drag to a third of their speed through the side tunnels — which is what makes a tunnel an escape route rather than a trap.

One rule is deliberately not the arcade's. The original game eventually withdraws the power pellet altogether. That is fair on a 28 × 31 board with long escape runs; on a 19 × 19 one it removes the player's only tool and the game becomes unwinnable. Here the frightened timer shrinks from seven seconds down to a floor of two, and stays there.

Positions are held in twelfths of a tile, and every speed in the game divides twelve. That is what guarantees a character always lands exactly on a tile centre, which is the only place a turn is allowed — a speed that did not divide twelve would eventually step over an alignment point and leave you unable to turn for the rest of the level.

04

Devices and data

Watches: 19 models on Connect IQ 3.1 or later — Forerunner 255, 255S, 265, 265S, 745, 955 and 965; fēnix 7, 7S and 7X; epix (Gen 2); Venu 2, 2S, 2 Plus, 3 and 3S; and vívoactive 4, 4S and 5. The board is laid out from the screen's own dimensions, so a new watch needs no new artwork — it has been geometry-checked at 416 × 416, 360 × 360 and 260 × 260.

Permissions: none. The app declares no permissions at all.

Data: no GPS, no network, no account, no activity recorded, nothing collected. Your score exists on the screen until you start the next game.
