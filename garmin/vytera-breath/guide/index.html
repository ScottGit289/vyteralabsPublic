User Guide

1. Before you start

This app is a training tool, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat anything. The app shows an unskippable version of this before your first session.

Three rules, every time
  • Never practise breath retention in or near water. Shallow-water blackout gives no warning and happens to strong swimmers.
  • Never practise while driving or operating anything.
  • Sit or lie down — never stand, because a faint while standing is a head injury rather than a moment of dizziness.

Speak to a doctor before using breath-hold or hyperventilation protocols if you are pregnant, or if you have a cardiovascular condition, high or low blood pressure, a history of fainting, epilepsy or any seizure disorder, a respiratory condition including asthma or COPD, a history of stroke or aneurysm, or if you are recovering from surgery.

Stop immediately if you feel faint, unwell, numb, or in pain at any point.

Breathing protocols can produce dizziness, tingling in the hands and face, and muscle tightening. These are expected effects of low carbon dioxide and pass. Losing consciousness is not an expected effect: if it happens, stop and seek medical advice before practising again.

The app also watches you while you practise. If your heart rate drops below your check-in floor, or the sensor loses your pulse during a hold, it plays the one vibration pattern it reserves for that — long, short, long, at full strength however low you have set the intensity — and asks you to check in.

You will be asked to acknowledge this twice, and only twice. Once on first launch, covering everything above. Then once more, separately, the first time you start a protocol that includes a breath-hold — because the risks that matter for a hold are not the risks of box breathing, and you should not have to remember a warning you accepted months earlier. Both are recorded on the watch, so neither returns; closing the second one without accepting cancels the session rather than starting it.

2. The eight protocols

Each protocol starts from a conservative default and is adjustable on the watch under Configure. The ranges below are the limits the app enforces.

CO2 toleranceThe same hold with shrinking rest, or a growing hold with fixed rest.8 rounds (4–12), hold 60s (15–180s) growing by 0–20s, first rest 120s (15–180s) shrinking by 0–30s to a floor of 15s (10–120s). Hold on full lungs or empty.
Box breathingEqual inhale, hold, exhale, hold.20 rounds (4–60), 4s a phase (3–8s).
ResonanceSlow breathing near the rate at which heart rate and breath line up.5.5 breaths a minute (4.5–7.0), 10 minutes (5–20), inhale 40% of the breath (35–50%).

A phase set to zero seconds is simply skipped, which is how the custom builder produces three-phase patterns as well as four.

3. Running a session

StepWhat to do
1Open Vytera Breath. Home shows the chosen protocol, today's target if the protocol has one, and your last session.
2To change protocol or adjust it, press MENU → Protocol or Configure. On Configure, START opens a row for editing and UP/DOWN change it.
3Sit or lie down. Press START. The app gives you a settle phase — breathe normally through it.
4Follow the vibrations. The screen shows one word and one number, but you do not need it: put your wrist down and close your eyes.
5In an open-ended hold, press START when you are done. Everywhere else START pauses and resumes.
6At the end, the save screen shows your rounds, how well you came back down, and lets you rate how it felt. START saves, BACK discards.

4. The controls

ButtonAction
STARTIn an open-ended hold: end the hold. Everywhere else: pause, and resume. One button, no mode to remember, and no way to end a hold by reaching for pause.
UP / DOWNTurn the page of the session screen. On Configure, change the value you are editing.
MENUDuring a session: resume, vibration-only, skip phase, finish now. On Home: start, configure, protocol, settings, about.
BACKEnds the protocol but keeps the work — you go to the save screen rather than losing the session. It is also what guarantees the sensors and the recording are shut down cleanly.

5. What the vibrations mean

The patterns were designed so that direction is felt rather than counted. You should not have to remember which pattern means what.

PatternWhat it means
RisingInhale. The cue grows in intensity, the way the breath does.
FallingExhale.
Staccato tapsHold. Categorically different from a breath cue, because a hold is a categorically different thing.
A single quiet pulseStill here — the periodic reassurance during a long hold.
Counting tapsThe end of a timed hold is approaching.
Long – short – longCheck in. Your heart rate has gone below the floor, or the sensor lost your pulse during a hold. It is the only pattern of this shape, and the only one that ignores your intensity setting.

6. Breath-holds, and when they end

A hold ends in one of three ways.

  • You end it. Press START. In an open-ended hold that is what the button does.
  • The clock ends it, where you set a target.
  • Your heart rate ends it, if you switch on End hold on HR rise. The hold ends when your heart rate climbs eight beats above its lowest point in that hold — the rise that comes with the urge to breathe — rather than at a number you chose beforehand.

The heart-rate rule is deliberately inert for the first twenty seconds of a hold. Wrist optical heart rate lags the real signal by several seconds and is still settling at the start; an early rise is as likely to be an artefact as a genuine response. Waiting costs you nothing, and ending a hold on noise teaches the wrong thing.

A missing heart rate never ends a hold early. If the sensor drops out, the hold falls through to the absolute cap instead — losing the sensor should not be indistinguishable from succeeding.

Whatever you manage, the app will not prescribe a hold longer than three minutes. That ceiling is not a setting.

7. What it tells you afterwards

The save screen reports, in plain words, how well you came back down. It is built from two components, each of which can be explained in a sentence:

  • The drop — how far your heart rate fell below where it started, worth up to 60 points at 6 per beat. A session that drove your heart rate up is marked down, but not bottomlessly.
  • The return — up to 40 points for coming back within five beats of your baseline, sliding to nothing across a two-minute window.
It saysMeaning

Not enough dataThe watch could not give enough heart rate to judge it. Nothing is invented to fill the gap.

It is a deliberately unclever score. A single opaque number derived from wrist HRV would look more scientific and mean less.

8. Targets and progression

Retention targets come from your own history rather than from a table. The rule is dull on purpose:

  • Today's target is the rolling median of your last five recorded holds. A median ignores the one heroic session that would otherwise become your new baseline.
  • It steps up by 5%, and only when your last two sessions both finished a hold without a heart-rate spike. Progression follows calm sessions, not brave ones.
  • You can override the suggestion whenever you like, and the override is what Home then shows.
  • Both are bounded by the three-minute ceiling regardless.

Settings → Reset progression clears the session history the targets are built from.

There is deliberately no units setting. Nothing in this app is measured in kilometres, miles, metres or feet — only in seconds, beats per minute and milliseconds, none of which differ between metric and imperial. A units toggle would have been a control that changed nothing.

10. What lands in Garmin Connect

A saved session is written to your watch as a breathwork activity — meditation, with a breathing sub-sport — so the summary you get fits what you did, rather than being a generic one with average speed and distance in it. Your watch syncs it the same way it syncs everything else. The app has no network of its own and uploads nothing.

On a watch whose software does not carry the breathing sub-sport, the activity records as meditation without it. Nothing is lost but the label: the laps, the graphs and the session name are all still there.

  • Duration and the heart-rate graph, from the standard record stream.
  • One lap per round on round-based protocols, so hold times read straight off the lap table even if nothing custom renders. Continuous protocols do not get a lap per breath, which would make the table useless.
  • Developer fields graphing breathing phase, breath rate, variability and the coherence band.
  • Up to two minutes of recording after your last breath, so the recovery is on the graph rather than cut off at the end of the protocol.

11. When something is wrong

The coherence indicator reads --. Your watch exposes neither a variability figure of its own nor beat-to-beat intervals. The app says so rather than inventing a number.

The check-in alert fired and you feel fine. It fires on a heart rate below your floor or on the sensor losing your pulse — a loose strap will do the second. Tighten the watch a finger's width above the wrist bone, and raise the floor in Settings if your resting rate is genuinely low.

No vibration at all. Check Vibration is not set to Off in the app, then check the watch's own Settings → System → Sound & Vibe.

Anything else: support@vyteralabs.com, with your watch model and the software version from Settings → System → About.
