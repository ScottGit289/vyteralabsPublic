1. Before anything else: the right muscle

This decides whether the next three months are worth anything. Most men who get no benefit from pelvic floor exercises are exercising the wrong thing, and weeks of diligent work on your buttocks will not change your continence.

The muscle is the one you would use to stop yourself passing wind, while at the same time drawing your penis in and shortening it. It is a lift and a tightening inwards, never a push down. In front of a mirror, a correct squeeze draws the base of the penis in slightly and lifts the scrotum.

What should not move: your buttocks and thighs stay still, your stomach does not brace hard, and your breathing does not stop. If you cannot talk while holding, you are bracing rather than lifting.

Two warnings. Do not use stopping your urine flow as the exercise — once, to work out which muscles are involved, is fine, but NHS guidance is consistent that regularly stopping and starting your flow can interfere with normal bladder emptying. And if you cannot feel anything, that is common soon after surgery: it is not permanent, and it is the main reason to ask for a referral to a men's health physiotherapist.

The watch cannot help you with this part. It times the exercise; it has no way of knowing which muscle you used. The iPhone guide covers finding it at greater length, with the published sources named — and the iPhone app itself is free on the UK App Store.

2. Setting your stage

Press the menu button, choose Stage, and pick where you are. This is the most important answer you give the app, because it sets your starting programme.

Changing the stage on the watch resets the programme to that stage's starting point. If a physiotherapist has given you different numbers, set the stage first and then edit the numbers, not the other way round.

3. Doing a set, and the buttons

From the home screen, press Select — the start button on your watch — and the set begins.

  1. Get ready — a few seconds to settle and find the muscle.
  2. Squeeze — hold for your set number of seconds. A long buzz starts it.
  3. Relax — let go completely. A short buzz. The rest matters as much as the hold: a muscle that never fully releases does not recover between repetitions.
  4. Shake it off — a short break between the long squeezes and the quick ones.
  5. Quick and let go — a fast, firm squeeze and an immediate full release, repeated. These train the muscle to react when you cough, sneeze, laugh or stand up.

Breathe normally throughout. Holding your breath is the most common mistake and it recruits the wrong muscles.

There is no confirmation when you press back. That is deliberate. Squeezing until the muscle gives out and stopping is correct technique, so a part set is recorded, counts towards the day, and the summary says so. If you genuinely started one by mistake, the summary's menu will undo it.

4. While the catheter is in

If your stage is Catheter still in, the home screen says On hold and pressing Select opens the menu rather than starting a set. Standard UK guidance after a prostatectomy is to begin active pelvic floor exercises once the catheter has been removed, and your team will tell you when.

Two ways out, both in the menu:

  • My catheter is out — moves you to the "catheter just out" programme.
  • Told to start now — an explicit override. Use it only if your surgeon, nurse or physiotherapist has specifically told you to exercise with the catheter in.

Common UK guidance caps the exercise at ten squeezes of ten seconds. Past that, more repetitions is not the goal — consistency is.

Reset programme in the menu puts the numbers back to your stage's starting point. Erase record deletes every set you have logged; it cannot be undone.

6. The record

The home screen shows sets done against your daily target, and the run of days you have kept going. Progress in the menu adds your best run, and how much of the last seven and twenty-eight days you actually completed.

The run of days counts back from today — or from yesterday if you have not started today, so it does not appear to break before the day is out.

Nothing here is uploaded, and nothing is written as an activity: there is no FIT file and no entry in Garmin Connect. The record lives in the watch's own storage. If you want something to take to an appointment, the iPhone app — free on the UK App Store — exports a plain summary and CSVs of your sets and bladder diary.

7. When to stop reading this and call someone

Speak to a clinician, not to us, if:
  • Leaking is getting worse, or has not improved by around three months after surgery.
  • You have pain, blood in your urine, or a burning sensation.
  • You cannot pass urine at all — that is urgent.
  • You cannot tell whether you are squeezing the right muscle.

A men's health physiotherapist can check your technique with a physical examination. No app can. Asking for that referral is the single highest-value thing you can do.
