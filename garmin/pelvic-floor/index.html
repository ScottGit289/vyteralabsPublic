Pelvic floor exercises are the one thing that reliably helps with leaking after prostate surgery, and almost nobody does them often enough. This times the set, buzzes at every change, and counts the day — so it can be done with your arm down, in a meeting, on a train, without anybody knowing.

01

What it does

One set is a block of long squeezes, each followed by a rest, then a block of quick squeezes. That is how a men's health physiotherapist prescribes it, and it is what the app counts out: get ready, squeeze, relax, squeeze, relax, and on to the quick ones.

Times the whole set

A ring counts each phase down with the seconds left and which repetition you are on. Nothing to read if you would rather not look — the ring and the buzz carry it.

Buzzes at every change

A long buzz to squeeze, a short one to let go, a double at the end. Different enough to follow with the watch out of sight. Vibration only by default; tones and silent are there if you want them.

Counts the day

Sets done against your daily target, the run of days you have kept going, and how much of the last seven and twenty-eight days you actually completed.

Waits while the catheter is in

UK guidance after a prostatectomy is to begin active pelvic floor exercises once the catheter has come out. Say the catheter is still in and the app holds the programme — with an explicit override if your team has told you otherwise.

02

Stopping early still counts

Soon after surgery the muscle tires within seconds. If it gives out at the fourth squeeze of eight, that is correct technique and a set worth recording — not a failure to be hidden.

Press back and the app keeps what you did, counts it towards the day, and says so in as many words. There is deliberately no are you sure: the discouraging version of this app is the one that throws away a part set, and men stop using discouraging apps.

The same honesty runs through it. The app never claims to know whether you squeezed the right muscle, because it cannot. That needs a person.

03

Your programme, not a generic one

Pick where you are — before surgery, catheter still in, catheter just out, building strength, keeping it going, or no surgery at all — and the app starts you on a conservative programme drawn from UK patient guidance for that stage. Short holds and long rests early on are not caution for its own sake: a set you cannot finish teaches you nothing.

If a physiotherapist gave you numbers, use theirs. Hold length, rest length, repetitions, quick squeezes and sets a day are all editable, and the stage, the sets a day and the cue style can be changed on the watch itself without reaching for a phone.

04

The iPhone app does the rest

This is the watch half of Anchor, which is out now on the UK App Store, free. The two run the identical programme — the same stages, the same starting numbers, the same timeline. Do a set on either.

What the watch deliberately leaves out is what a watch is bad at. The bladder diary needs typing. The export for your appointment — a plain summary plus CSVs of your sets and diary — needs somewhere to send it. The reading, eight short articles each naming its published sources, needs a screen. All three are on the iPhone app, which is also free.

05

Devices and data

Devices: round Garmin watches from fēnix 6, Forerunner 245 and Venu onwards — including fēnix 7 and 8, epix 2, Forerunner 255, 745, 945 and 955, Venu 2, vívoactive 4, enduro, MARQ 2 and tactix 7. The final list is fixed at release and will be shown here and on the store page.

Permissions: none. Not one. The app requests no network, no position, no sensors and no background service. That is unusual enough to be worth stating plainly — it cannot send anything anywhere, because it has no way to.

Nothing is recorded. No activity, no FIT file, no timeline entry. Your settings and the count of sets per day live in the watch's own storage and nowhere else.

Free. Nothing to unlock, no adverts, no subscription. The men who most need this are three weeks past an operation and not in the mood to weigh up a paid medical app.

06

Not a medical device

Anchor Pelvic Floor times an exercise programme and keeps a record. It does not assess, diagnose or treat anything, it cannot tell whether your recovery is on track, and it cannot check that you are contracting the right muscle — that needs a person, usually a men's health physiotherapist or a continence nurse.

The programme your clinician gives you takes priority over anything in this app. If leaking is getting worse, has not improved by around three months after surgery, or you have pain or blood in your urine, speak to your team rather than to an app. If you cannot pass urine at all, that is urgent.
