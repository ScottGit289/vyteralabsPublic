Anchor · User guide

How to use Anchor.

Three things: it times your exercises, it reminds you, and it keeps a record you can hand to a professional. That is the whole app.

Free · iPhone · iOS 17 or later About 8 minutes to read
01

Before anything else: find the muscle

The step that decides everything Start here

Most men who get no benefit from pelvic floor exercises are exercising the wrong thing. Weeks of diligent work on your buttocks will not change your continence.

The muscle you want is the one you would use to stop yourself passing wind, while at the same time drawing your penis in and shortening it. It is a lift and a tightening inwards — never a push down. In front of a mirror, a correct squeeze draws the base of the penis in slightly and lifts the scrotum.

What should not move

  • Your buttocks and thighs should stay still.
  • Your stomach should not brace hard.
  • Your breathing should not stop. If you cannot talk while holding, you are bracing, not lifting.

Two honest warnings

  • Do not use stopping your urine flow as the exercise. Once, to work out which muscles are involved, is fine. NHS guidance is consistent that regularly stopping and starting your flow can interfere with normal bladder emptying.
  • If you cannot feel anything, that is common — especially in the first weeks after surgery. It is not failure and it is not permanent. Ask your GP or your surgical team to refer you to a men’s health physiotherapist, who can check what you are doing. It is the single highest-value thing you can do.

The Learn tab in the app carries a fuller article on this, with every source named.

Five short screens on first launch. The one that matters is the first.

If a physiotherapist gave you different numbers, use theirs — Settings has a full plan editor for every value.

03

Doing a set

Tap Start a set on the Today tab. The ring counts each phase and tells you what to do.

  1. Get ready — a few seconds to settle and find the muscle.
  2. Squeeze and lift — hold for your set number of seconds.
  3. Relax — let go completely. The rest matters as much as the hold; a muscle that never fully releases does not recover between repetitions.
  4. Shake it off — a short break between the long squeezes and the quick ones.
  5. Quick squeezes — a fast, firm squeeze and an immediate full release. These train the muscle to react in time when you cough, sneeze, laugh or stand up — the moments when leaks actually happen.
  6. Done.

Three things worth knowing

  • Breathe normally throughout. Holding your breath is the most common mistake, and it recruits the wrong muscles.
  • Stopping early still counts. If the muscle gives out at repetition four of eight, that is correct technique, not failure. Tap stop and choose “Stop and keep what I’ve done”. Anchor records the repetitions you actually completed and shows it as a part set, not a miss.
  • Add a note when you save. “Tired”, “easier today”, “after the long drive”. Notes are carried into the summary you can share, and they are the detail a physiotherapist actually asks about.

Cues are vibration only by default — nothing audible, so a set on a train is nobody else’s business. Settings › During a set also offers tones, spoken cues (which work with the phone in your pocket and the screen off), or complete silence.

04

If your catheter is still in

Anchor holds the timed programme rather than starting it. Standard UK guidance after a prostatectomy is to begin active pelvic floor exercises once the catheter has been removed. Your team will tell you when.

The Learn section and the bladder diary stay fully open in the meantime.

Two buttons sit on the hold card. “My catheter has been removed” moves you onto the “Catheter just out” programme. “I have been told to start now” is an explicit override with a confirmation step — use it only if your surgeon, nurse or physiotherapist has specifically told you to exercise with the catheter in.

05

The bladder diary

This is the part men skip, and the part clinicians want. Three honest days tells a continence nurse more than a month of describing it from memory.

Quick log on the Today tab records the four common events in a single tap: a leak, a visit to the toilet, a pad change, a drink. Everything else lives on the Diary tab, where you can pick a day and add or edit entries in full.

  • Drink — with a volume in millilitres
  • Passed urine — with a volume
  • Leak — with how much (a few drops, damp, wet through) and what you were doing (cough or sneeze, lifting or bending, standing up, walking or exercise, could not get there in time, not sure)
  • Pad change
  • Sudden urge

Plus a free note on any entry — a long drive, a heavy day, a change in medication.

You do not need to fill in every box. Leaks and pad changes alone are useful.

06

Progress, and the appointment

The Progress tab charts sets completed and leaks recorded over 7, 28 or 90 days against your daily target. History lists every set with the notes you wrote, and those notes stay editable.

Export my data — on the Progress tab, or in Settings › Your data — builds three files:

  • a plain-text summary: your stage and programme, sets completed against sets prescribed, adherence as a percentage, sets finished in full, current streak, leaks and pad changes per day, the trend across the period, your most common leak triggers, and your notes
  • a CSV of every set
  • a CSV of your bladder diary

It opens the standard iOS share sheet. You choose the destination — email it to your physiotherapist, save it to Files, print it. Anchor has no destination of its own and uploads nothing.

Take the summary with you. “Here is 28 days” is a far better opening than trying to remember.

07

Stepping up

Anchor offers one extra second of hold, or one extra repetition, only after six of the last seven days ended in a full set. It asks at most once a week, and it stops at ten squeezes of ten seconds — the ceiling in common UK guidance.

Past that point, more repetitions is not the goal. Consistency is.

Accept with Step up, decline with Not yet, or set the numbers yourself in Settings › Edit programme at any time.

08

Settings worth knowing about

  • Stage — change it as you move through recovery.
  • Edit programme — hold length, rest length, repetitions, quick squeezes, sets per day, preparation time.
  • Reminders — add, remove or move each one.
  • Discreet notifications — on by default. A reminder says “Anchor — time for your set” and nothing more on a lock screen.
  • Cues — vibration only, tones, spoken, or screen only.
  • Keep screen on during a set — stops the phone locking mid-set.
  • Appearance — match device, always light, or always dark. Dark is easier on the eyes for a set in the night.
  • Safety and limitations — what Anchor does and, more importantly, what it does not.
  • Erase everything — removes every set, every diary entry and your settings from this phone. It cannot be undone.

The home-screen widget

Add Anchor’s widget for a ring showing sets done today against your target, and your streak. It reads the same on-device record the app does.

Learn

Eight short articles: finding the right muscles, how the exercises work, what to expect after prostate surgery, whether this actually works, five common mistakes, bladder habits that help, when to ask for help, and the effects beyond leaking. Every article names the published sources it draws on — Prostate Cancer UK, Macmillan, NHS trust patient information, and the Cochrane Library. Where the evidence is uncertain, Anchor says so rather than rounding it up into a promise.

09

When to stop reading this and call someone

Speak to a clinician, not to us Important
  • Leaking is getting worse rather than better, or has not improved by around three months after surgery.
  • You have pain, blood in your urine, or a burning sensation.
  • You cannot pass urine at all — that is urgent.
  • You cannot tell whether you are squeezing the right muscle.

A men’s health physiotherapist can check your technique with a physical examination. Anchor cannot, and no app can.
